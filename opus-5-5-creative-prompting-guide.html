Lines you leave out still get drawn. The model fills them with its most average guess, which is why vague prompts return scenes that feel familiar. This guide is about deciding those lines yourself.

The 60-second version

Skim mode is on. You are seeing the essentials only.

flag

Describe the finish line, not the route

State the outcome, the inputs, the limits, the evidence you want, and when to stop. Opus 5.5 plans its own steps better than a hand-written recipe, so spend your words on what "finished" looks like.

speed

Use the effort dial, delete "think hard"

Opus 5.5 always thinks and defaults to medium. Raise effort only after medium misses details you listed, instead of writing instructions about thinking. Sonnet 5.5 defaults to high and can skip thinking at lower levels (see Effort).

block

Ban defaults by name

"Avoid a generic look" trades one default for another. List the exact looks you refuse and extend the list after each render. Anthropic's frontend-design skill names five default clusters: cream with a terracotta accent, near-black with one acid accent, broadsheet rules and columns, identical rounded cards with the same soft shadow, and template chrome such as tracked capitals, middle-dot meta strings and a trailing arrow. For games, add a HUD of glass cards framing the view.

photo_camera

Give it eyes and ears

Name the frames to capture and what to check in each. For sound, have it render to WAV and read loudness and a spectrogram, because it cannot listen.

travel_explore

Let it study before it builds

Opus 5.5 starts work fast. On loose briefs, ask it to research the real thing first (a real map, real wave footage, the original controls). Anthropic measured better results when it explored before acting.

straighten

Numbers over adjectives

"AAA" is a mood. 60 fps at 1440p on an M-series laptop is a spec. So are resolution, palette size, loop length and frame budget.

science

Give feedback in physics

When iterating, say where it breaks, what you see, and what should physically happen. "Looks cartoony" gives the model nothing to aim at.

Most of these come from Anthropic's Opus 5.5 prompting guide and frontend-design skill. "Numbers over adjectives" and "Give feedback in physics" are patterns from the community prompts later on this page.

Anatomy of a build brief

stacksStrong creative prompts cover nine layers. Click a layer to see the lines that carry it.

The example takes the one-line Halloween diorama request ("rainy night, warm firelight, 1080p, loops for hours") and writes out every decision the short version left to chance. Write the layers in this order: what you are shipping first, how to prove it works last.

Build one self-contained HTML file with vanilla JS and Canvas 2D. No libraries, no network requests, no text anywhere on screen.

A pixel-art Halloween street corner on a cold rainy night: a crooked house with two lit windows, a pumpkin patch, an iron fence, a lamp post, and a small bonfire in the foreground.

The bonfire is the hero: a three-layer flame, embers that rise and fade, and warm light that flickers across the wet stones around it.

Loop exactly every 60 seconds with no visible seam. Poses step at 10 fps while particles update at 60 Hz. A cat walks the fence once per loop.

28-colour palette: 16 cold blues and violets for the night, 12 warm oranges for fire and windows. The warm pockets are the brightest, highest-contrast areas.

Render at 320×180 and scale ×6 to exactly 1920×1080 with image-rendering: pixelated. Rain as 1px streaks in three depth layers with splashes on the ground. Soft falloff uses Bayer dithering only.

Hold 60 fps for ten hours: fixed timestep, pooled particles, zero allocation in the loop, no memory growth.

Before finishing, capture frames at 0 s, 15 s, 30 s and 59.9 s, compare the first and last frame for the seam, and fix anything that pops, clips or floats.

Reply with the file, the four captures and two sentences on what they show.

One line, one visible change

tuneTurn each spec line on and watch what it does to the same little scene.

With every switch off you get what an unspecified prompt tends to produce: soft edges, smooth tweening and a gradient sky. Nothing is wrong with it, it just is not pixel art. Four short lines are the difference.

Starter prompt
Build one self-contained HTML file (vanilla JS, Canvas 2D, no libraries, no network) showing [subject] as a looping pixel-art animation.

Canvas: draw at [320×180] logical pixels, scale by the largest integer that fits, centred, image-rendering: pixelated. Snap every draw to whole pixels. No gradients or blur. Soft falloff uses 4×4 Bayer dithering.
Palette: [24] fixed colours. Roles: [sky steps], [skin and cloth], [3 bright accent colours], [2 outline darks].
Character: about [24×32 px], built from rects and pixel runs. Pose parameters: [list]. Animate them smoothly, then quantise poses to [10] fps inside a 60 Hz loop.
States: [IDLE 2 s] then [ACTION 1.5 s] then [RESOLVE 1 s], eased between keyframes, looping with no seam.
Particles: one preallocated pool of [300], zero allocation inside the loop, fixed 60 Hz timestep.
Evidence: capture a frame in every state plus the loop point, compare first and last frame, fix shimmer, clipping and pops.
Done when it holds 60 fps on [device] and each state is identifiable in its captured frame at 1×. Reply in two sentences and attach the captures.

Playable builds. The most-shared one-shot game prompts on both models spell out an objective, controls, feedback, scoring and a restart state.

check_circleNail these

  • The core loop in one sentence, and the kind of fun it targets
  • Control feel as numbers: speed, acceleration, inertia, jump height
  • Title, play, fail, win, restart without a reload
  • Camera rules: spring arm, collision, never inside walls or below ground
  • Physics on a fixed timestep, jump set by height and time to apex
  • A self-playtest with screenshots of each state
  • A grey blockout that plays well before any art
  • Enemy AI with a debug overlay: state, vision, path
  • A juice pass only after the loop is fun
  • An engine the agent can build, test and screenshot from a terminal (see Engines and libraries)

errorWhere it breaks

  • Floaty or laggy controls
  • The camera clipping through geometry
  • Many features and no loop worth repeating
  • Levels built at real-world scale, so they feel empty
  • Enemies with instant detection and perfect aim
  • No fail or restart path
  • A HUD of four to six glass cards framing the view. The first screen should be playable, with information second.
Starter prompt
Build a complete browser game in one HTML file using [three.js r186 / Canvas 2D]. Inline every library (no CDN at runtime) and generate every model, texture and sound in code. No paid or hosted generators, even if API keys exist.

Fun target: [challenge / discovery / expression / ...] (see Game design).
Core loop: the player [does X] to [achieve Y] while [avoiding Z]. Pacing: the core action repeats every [5 to 30] s, something new appears every [1 to 5] min, pressure starts in the first minute, and the first real decision comes within 30 s.
Controls: [WASD + mouse]. Feel targets: top speed [n], reaches it in [0.4 s], turn inertia [light], jump defined by height [2.2 m] and time to apex [0.4 s], coyote time and jump buffer [100 ms]. Physics on a fixed timestep with interpolated rendering. Input responds within one frame.
Touch, if mobile ships: the same actions as the keyboard, targets of at least 44 px, touch-action set on the canvas only, and input released on pointercancel, lostpointercapture and blur.
Structure: title, play, fail, win, and restart without reload. Score plus one difficulty curve that rises over [3 minutes].
Camera: third-person on a spring arm at [6 m] with a collision cast, never inside walls or below ground, no bob with the walk cycle, shake on a separate layer.
Level: grey blockout first, sized from the controller's metrics (doors at least 2× player width), a critical path with numbered beats, one loop that returns as a shortcut, one landmark visible from most of the path. Art only after the blockout plays well. Audio synthesised, [M] mutes.
Enemies: [state machine / behaviour tree] with a vision cone, hearing and last known position, navmesh or grid A* pathfinding, a visible awareness telegraph before attacking, and a debug overlay on [F1]. All randomness seeded.
Juice, once the loop works: hitstop, trauma-based shake, spring squash, layered hit sounds, all scaled by an intensity value, with a reduce-shake toggle.
Performance: 60 fps on [a named mid-range laptop], drop render scale below 50 fps.
Test hooks: expose window.__GAME_TEST__ with seed(n), setState(name), pause() and hideDebugUi(), so tests reach every state without playing to it.
Evidence: a scripted bot plays [3] seeded runs in Playwright (channel "chromium", one worker). A run passes when it renders over 100 frames, moves the player, raises the score, never goes [5] s without movement or progress, and logs zero console errors. Rerun with a 300 ms reaction delay to check the game stays winnable. Then reach a win and a fail yourself and screenshot every state.
Done when the bot runs pass, every state has a screenshot, and the 10-second screenshot shows the goal, the player and the first threat without any text. Stop after [30] turns and list what is still open.

Ambient loops and video covers that run for hours. The risk is not the first minute, it is hour six.

check_circleNail these

  • Loop length and a first-frame versus last-frame seam test
  • Every periodic motion uses a period that divides the loop
  • Warm versus cold palette roles, with a count for each
  • Exact output size: 320×180 scaled ×6 is 1920×1080
  • A soak test for memory and particle counts

  • Memory creeping up over hours
  • Every layer repeating in sync, so the loop feels short
  • Flicker harsh enough to tire the eye
  • Non-integer scaling that blurs pixels

Engines and libraries

constructionPick by where it ships, then by what the agent can build, run, test and screenshot from a terminal. Text files and a headless command line give the agent a fast loop. Binary assets and editor-only steps leave it guessing.

Web or engine? A web library wins when the result must load from a link with no install, sit inside a site, or become a coded video. An engine wins for large content, native performance, console targets or an existing C# or C++ team. Godot 4 web export uses the WebGL 2 Compatibility renderer, needs cross-origin isolation headers for threads, and cannot export C# projects. Unreal has no official web export, only Pixel Streaming from a server.

What makes an engine agent-friendly

Text over binaryGodot .tscn and web code diff cleanly and can be edited directly. Unity scenes must go through the editor. Unreal assets need the editor, Python or MCP.
Headless build and testEvery step the agent needs (import, build, test, export) runs from one command with an exit code and a log it can read.
Deterministic captureFrames rendered at a fixed step, not recorded live: seek(t) on the web, Godot's Movie Maker (--write-movie out.png --fixed-fps 30 --quit-after 300, without --headless), a fixed timestep with HighResShot in Unreal.
An editor bridge, used sparinglyMCP servers run code inside the editor. Unreal's listens on localhost with no authentication, and Blender's Lab server runs model-written Python unguarded. Keep them on a dev machine and off shared networks.
Pinned version, migration guide at handModels have seen far more old code than new. Name the version in the brief and point the agent at the migration guide (Godot 3 to 4, Phaser 3 to 4, R3F 8 to 9, three.js releases).
Prove the rendererA web build can fall back to WebGL silently. Assert which backend ran, for example renderer.backend.isWebGPUBackend in three.js, so a fallback screenshot does not pass as a WebGPU test.

Headless Chromium on a machine with no GPU: WebGPU needs a secure context (localhost counts) and --enable-unsafe-webgpu, which gives a SwiftShader adapter. Since Chrome 139, WebGL no longer falls back to SwiftShader silently, so add --use-angle=swiftshader --enable-unsafe-swiftshader. On a Linux GPU, Chrome documents --headless=new --use-angle=vulkan --enable-features=Vulkan --disable-vulkan-surface --enable-unsafe-webgpu. Keep Playwright's Chromium current, since an old browser build can reject newer three.js WebGPU calls.

Game design

extensionDesign decisions for the player to make, then build only the features those decisions need. Name the kind of fun you are after, prototype the smallest loop that produces it, and let playtests decide which rules stay.

Mechanics make dynamics, dynamics make the feeling

The MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek) splits a game into mechanics (the rules you write), dynamics (what happens when players meet those rules) and aesthetics (what the player feels). You can only change the mechanics. The player experiences it from the other end. Pick the feeling first, then work backwards.

Prompt line

      

      
The eight kinds of fun come from the MDA paper, developed for the GDC Game Design and Tuning Workshop (2001 to 2004): sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression and submission. The example games are illustrations, not claims about their designers' intent.


      
Ideas that hold up

      

        
Core loopThe action the player repeats most, stated in one sentence with verbs: "dash through a room, grab loot, spend it between runs".

        
Interesting decisionsSid Meier's test for a mechanic: does it ask the player to choose between options that are both attractive? If one option always wins, it is a chore.

        
Fun as learningRaph Koster's argument: play is the brain enjoying learning a pattern. When the pattern is mastered or unreadable, boredom sets in. Keep adding readable twists.

        
FeelSteve Swink defines feel as real-time control of objects in a simulated space, emphasised by polish. It lives in numbers and feedback, which is why the juice and physics sections exist.

        
Risk and rewardBigger rewards sit behind bigger risks the player can see and choose.

        
Difficulty as a curveTeach, test, then twist. Ramp challenge in waves with rests, as in the level design pacing curve.

        
Depth from few rulesFewer mechanics that interact beat many that don't. Tynan Sylvester (RimWorld) builds emotional experiences from elegant mechanics, fiction and pacing working together.

        
Readable failureWhen players lose, they should know why and want to try again at once.

Value structureDecide which things are lightest and darkest before choosing hues. Gameplay objects get the most contrast against their background.

        
Silhouette testEvery enemy, pickup and interactive object must read as a solid black shape at gameplay distance. If two read the same, redesign one.

Lock the style for generated assets

      

        
Style formulaOne 60 to 90 word paragraph in five blocks: rendering style, shape and line language, palette by role (the hero contrasts, hazards and pickups share one signal hue), light and mood, readability plus perspective. Paste it byte-identical into every asset prompt. Asset content, keying and sizes stay out of it.

        
Key colour for alphaMost image models cannot output transparency (GPT Image 2.5 and local Qwen-Image 2.1 can, and 2.1 is non-commercial). Pick the key away from the subject's hue: green for pink, purple and red subjects, magenta for green and teal ones, never cyan or blue. Check that small features (eyes, gems) survive keying, and check enclosed holes.

        
Scale contact sheetGive every asset a scale in hero units. Before approval, lay them all out at scale on the ground tile and look at them together.

        
Light from the formulaDerive the engine's light, fog and ambient from the light and mood block. New textures under old lighting look pasted on.

        
Anti-convergence ledgerRecord six identity axes of the last build: palette family, type pairing, hero composition, signature technique, UI shapes, corner language. The next build must differ on at least four.

        
Reference board rules10 to 25 images, each tagged with a role, a reason and one thing to take. Keep 2 or 3 anti-references per 4 to 8 positives, then reduce to 3 to 6 tagged references before generating.

        
Choosing a directionOffer 3 or 4 mutually exclusive options on one named axis, each with when it wins and when it fails. Ask for the pick first, then for a graft such as "C's light on A's composition".

        
Generating alternativesList 5 to 10 candidate directions with rough likelihoods, drop the most obvious ones, and build from the rest. The literal answer stays as a control.

      

      
The style formula, key-colour rule and anti-convergence ledger come from Higgsfield's game-stylization and review-rubric references. The board and choosing rules come from Scenario's inspiration skill. All of them work on either generation route.

  1. Text anchorA fixed identity block, plus a per-shot block for what may change
    2. 
        
  2. Approved referenceOne hero image every variation points to
    3. 
        
  3. Style referenceA swatch or frame that carries the look
    4. 
        
  4. Control mapPose, depth (DA3MONO) or edges to hold composition, on the local route
    5. 
        
  5. Trained LoRAOne per character, plus a separate style LoRA, on the local route

Baseline plus deltaThe baseline prompt stays byte-identical across a set. Only the final clause changes.

        
Anchor to the heroEvery variation references the same approved hero. Chaining output to output compounds drift. A lasting change, such as a new costume, becomes a new approved hero.

        
Reference beats descriptionOnce a reference image is supplied, stop describing the face in words. The two pull against each other and cause drift.

        
Seeds repeat one rollA seed reproduces a single image. It carries nothing across a set.

        
Control mapsStrength about 0.7 for depth or edges, 0.8 to 0.9 for pose. End control around 65% of the steps so detail can refine. Never extract a map from the attribute you are changing.

        
When to trainOne character across hundreds of images, or four or more identities in shared scenes. Use 5 to 15 images for a character, 10 to 20 for a style. Captions describe what should vary, never what must stay. The best epoch is rarely the last.

      


      
Gates and stop rules

      

        
Rubric firstWrite pass or fail lines before generating. A rubric written after seeing the batch inherits its flaws.

        
Verdicts, not vibesThe critic answers index: pass | fail, failed line. A vision model can check the lines, but it cannot prove an exact hex, font or spacing, so measure those in code.

        
Plausibility is on youQuality scorers catch artefacts, not impossible objects. In Scenario's tests a car with mismatched wheels scored 92 and passed. Add structural checks: counts, attachments, what carries the weight.

        
Cheapest fix firstA local defect gets a masked inpaint. A uniform grade or crop gets a code pass. Wrong content means editing the delta clause. Drift means tightening references. If everything fails, change the model.

        
One variable per roundRegenerate from the baseline each time, changing one thing.

        
Stop ruleA clean round ships. After three rounds without one, stop and report the best result with its flaws. A line that fails twice under two different fixes is evidence about the model.

        
Sweep video at 2 fpsA single sample missed a defect half a second away in Scenario's tests. Judge frame sheets, never only the first frame.

        
Log every nodeid, role, model, inputs, outputs and status (accepted, retried, rejected), so any output can be traced and rerun.

      

      
Comparing two models? Pre-register three to five pass or fail criteria, keep prompts and inputs byte-identical with the same output count, and turn prompt expansion off. stadia_controllerA level is a sequence of decisions in space. Plan the beats and the metrics, block it out in grey, walk it at player speed, and only then dress it. Agents jump straight to art unless the prompt stops them.

    2.

    3.

    4.

The five beats follow the teach, develop, twist, conclude structure Nintendo used in Super Mario 3D World. The bottom curve is the pacing: tension rises, releases at the vista, peaks at the finale.

        

      


      
The process pros follow

      
    
        
  1. Pre-productionGoal, references, a beat list, what the level teaches
    2. 
        
  2. Layout sketchCritical path, loops, landmarks, gates. Any drawing works.
    3. 
        
  3. Metrics playgroundA test map of every jump, door, stair and cover size
    4. 
        
  4. BlockoutGrey primitives on a grid sized to the player, prototype textures
    5. 
        
  5. Playtest at player speedWalk it with real gravity and collision, never fly the editor camera
    6. 
        
  6. Diverge and iterateMost blockouts do not survive the first playtest. That is the point.
    7. 
        
  7. Scripting and lightingEncounters, triggers, then light for wayfinding and mood
    8. 
        
  8. Art passSwap blocks for kit pieces with the same dimensions
    9. 
      

      
The sequence follows The Level Design Book's process. Its blockout method: sketch a layout, add a ground plane, a player-sized scale figure and walls, playtest, then diverge and repeat. It also notes that art-dependent narrative games (Firewatch, Untitled Goose Game) got more from an art-first vertical slice or location scouting than from a grey blockout.


      
Principles, as prompt vocabulary

      

        
Metrics firstPlayer size, speed, jump height and distance set every door, gap and room. Build the world to the metrics, never the reverse.

        
Critical path and beatsThe ideal route through the level, with numbered beats. Players will wander, so it is a plan, not a prediction.

        
PacingAlternate tension and release. Teach a mechanic safely, develop it, twist it, conclude.

        
Loops over dead endsCycles give multiple routes and shortcuts back. Unexplored generates dungeons from cycles because trees feel random and artificial.

        
Lock and keyA visible gate first, the key elsewhere, then the return. Gates create goals and structure exploration.

        
Landmarks and sightlinesSomething tall and distinct, visible from many places, so players build a mental map.

        
Over or aroundBreath of the Wild's triangle rule: shapes that hide what is behind them and ask the player to climb or circle, with points of interest at the peaks.

        
Desire linesCompare the path you drew with the path players actually take, and pave the one they want where it fits.

      

      
A useful counterweight: The Level Design Book argues against heavy-handed "guiding the player". Too much signposting and breadcrumbing makes a level feel like Disneyland. Aim for a readable space players can learn, not a path they are dragged along.

Both balls get the same jump every second. The left one integrates with each frame's delta, so jump heights change with frame rate. The right one steps at a fixed 120 Hz and interpolates for display, so every jump is identical on any machine.

        

      


      
The loop that fixes it

      
Fixed timestep with interpolation
const DT = 1 / 60;               // simulation step, never the frame delta
let acc = 0, prev = performance.now();
function frame(now) {
  let ft = (now - prev) / 1000; prev = now;
  ft = Math.min(ft, 0.25);       // avoid the spiral of death after a stall
  acc += ft;
  while (acc >= DT) { savePrevious(); step(DT); acc -= DT; }
  render(acc / DT);              // alpha: blend previous and current state
  requestAnimationFrame(frame);
}

      
This is Glenn Fiedler's accumulator from "Fix Your Timestep": clamp the frame time, step in fixed increments, then render a blend of the previous and current state using the leftover fraction.

Rows, not one big sheetOne generation per state, cut into frames by connected components. Asking for a whole grid in one image, or cutting fixed cells, drifts identity and overlaps poses. In sprite-gen's validation, 9 and 12 frame rows raised duplicates and extraction failures.

        
Deterministic keying beats matting hereOn a flat key, unmixing edges into despilled colour plus alpha left less key contamination than BiRefNet-lite or ViTMatte in sprite-gen's ground-truth test. Keep BiRefNet for photos and busy backgrounds.

        
Never pin each footIn a walk one foot lifts every step, so snapping each frame to its own lowest pixels makes the body lurch by a stride. Remove linear drift and use the mean foot line.

        
Count the strides by eyeA walk can repeat almost as well at one step as at a full stride, and pixels cannot tell the two apart. sprite-gen flags such a loop and stops the direction set until the agent looks at the frames and passes --cycles. Borrow Feel's vocabulary

      
More Mountains' Feel is a Unity asset, but its model is the clearest way to describe juice to any engine, including a Three.js or Canvas build. One player component holds a list of feedbacks, and each feedback does exactly one thing: a sound, a flash, a camera shake.

      

        
Feedback playerA named list per event (JumpFeedback, LandingFeedback). Code only calls play.

        
Parallel, pause, holding pauseEverything starts together until a pause. A holding pause waits for everything above it to finish.

        
IntensityOne number passed at play time scales every feedback. A light hit and a crit share one player.

        
TimingInitial delay, cooldown against spam, repeats, and chance per feedback.

        
RandomnessA random intensity and duration multiplier, so repeats never look identical.

        
Shakers and channelsFeedbacks broadcast, and listeners on a matching channel react. Split screens stay independent.

        
SpringsDamping plus frequency, then MoveTo or Bump. Spammable and additive, no duration to tune.

        
Global switchesAny feedback type can be disabled everywhere, which is how a "reduce screen shake" option works.

Seekable springs (motion.js)
// Damped spring from 0 to 1 as a pure function of time, so seek(t) stays deterministic.
export function spring(t, k = 170, c = 26) {
  if (t <= 0) return 0;
  const w = Math.sqrt(k), z = c / (2 * w);
  if (z >= 1) return 1 - Math.exp(-w * t) * (1 + w * t);   // treat overdamped as critical
  const wd = w * Math.sqrt(1 - z * z);
  return 1 - Math.exp(-z * w * t) * (Math.cos(wd * t) + (z * w / wd) * Math.sin(wd * t));
}

// A value that changes target over time: one spring per change, summed.
export function track(t, keys, k, c) {            // keys: [[time, value], ...] sorted by time
  let v = keys[0][1];
  for (let i = 1; i < keys.length; i++) v += (keys[i][1] - keys[i - 1][1]) * spring(t - keys[i][0], k, c);
  return v;
}

GPU compute, only when it pays

      
Three.js TSL exposes compute shaders with storage buffers, workgroupArray shared memory, workgroupBarrier, atomics and subgroup operations. Use them for large parallel workloads such as particles, simulation or culling, and measure the workgroup size and memory layout on target hardware instead of assuming. Advanced compute (atomics, workgroup memory, indirect draws) is WebGPU-only and plain compute is only partly supported on the WebGL 2 fallback, so a project that ships both needs a reduced path, feature-gated and tested separately.

      
WebGPU status, Sep 2026: on by default in Chrome and Edge on Windows, macOS and ChromeOS, Safari 26 on every Apple platform, and Firefox 141+ on Windows and 145+ on Apple Silicon Macs. Linux browsers and Firefox on Android still need the fallback. three.js r186 (Sep 2026) adds a TSL Gaussian-splat renderer and loader with glTF import, compileComputeAsync(), a SunLight with cascaded shadows and Object3D.dispose(). It also removes the minified builds and deprecates CommonJS, so single-file prompts should inline the ES module build at a pinned version.

Opus 5.5 posts progress as it works, and some of those updates end the turn with text instead of a tool call. A harness that treats that as "done" stops with work left. On the API those notes arrive as thinking blocks that are empty by default, so a text-only client looks silent. Set display: "updates" (beta) to see them, and after about five silent steps add a turn-scoped reminder to report. Anthropic's fix for early stops is to name the ones you do not want:

      

        
do_not_disturb_onStops to rule out
    
          
  • A summary that announces the next step and then ends
    • 
          
  • An offer to continue "unless you would prefer otherwise"
    • 
          
  • A list of decisions, none of which actually block the work
    • 
          
  • Pausing because a milestone felt like a good place to report
    • 
        

        
pan_toolStops to keep
    
          
  • Nothing can move forward without you
    • 
          
  • The blocker is deliberately protected (keys, paid APIs)
    • 
          
  • Anything destructive or irreversible
    • 
        

      

      

        
For builds that span several sessions, use the feature list and progress file from Multi-session builds. Pair either with a completion condition the harness can check. In Claude Code, /goal does this: a small model checks the condition after each turn. It only reads the transcript and never runs commands, so write a condition Claude's own output can show ("npm test exits 0", "all six screenshots attached and pass the checklist"), keep it under 4,000 characters, and add "or stop after 30 turns". Goals with background work check in every 30 minutes, set by CLAUDE_CODE_GOAL_CHECKIN_MINUTES. If you run your own loop, treat a turn that ends in text as a report, not completion: send one line naming the open items, and stop after two or three automatic continuations so a stuck run gets reviewed. A time signal also helps: Opus 5.5 paces work against lines like elapsed 340s / 1200s, and one sentence saying time matters speeds up agent teams.

        
Stop rule for creative builds
Keep going through every step that does not need me, and put status notes in the same message as your next action. Stop only when you cannot continue without my input, or before anything destructive. The task is done when [all four screenshots pass the checklist and the console is clean], or after [30] turns, whichever comes first. If you stop early, say which item is still open and what blocks it.

        
A numeric stop such as "five consecutive iterations with less than 5% improvement" works. Add how quality is measured, for example a per-pixel difference against reference frames under 1%, so "without sacrificing quality" is testable.

        
Change effort inside a run

        
Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 accept a per-message effort change in beta (header mid-conversation-output-config-2026-07-01): send a system message with empty content and output_config.effort, and the new level applies from the next user turn with the prompt cache intact. Changing the top-level effort between requests breaks the cache. Use it to scout at low, build at medium or high, and drop back for re-renders. Sonnet 5.5 with between_tools thinking rejects a mid-conversation change with a 400. It also rejects non-default temperature or top_p.

        
For long Opus 5.5 builds, Anthropic's 24 Sep advice keeps cached reads, which cost 60% less: pick the model at the start of the session, compact before you step away rather than after, and on an API key set the one-hour cache lifetime.

Traps and what to write instead

      
warningMost bad outputs trace back to one of these thirty-eight lines.

      

        
You wroteWhy it misfiresWrite this

        
          
"Make it AAA / Unreal quality"No observable criteria, so it cannot tell when it has arrived.Three things you would see in a screenshot, plus a frame budget.

          
"Avoid a generic AI look"Swaps one default style for another."Do not use [glass HUD cards framing the view, purple-pink gradients, cream and terracotta menus, tracked capitals with trailing arrows, glow on everything]."

          
"Think step by step" / "think hard"Opus 5.5 always thinks. Asking it to write out its reasoning can be declined as reasoning_extraction.Set effort. To read the reasoning, set display: "summarized". On Sonnet 5.5 at low or medium, "Think the problem through before you answer" is the documented exception.

          
"Iterate until perfect"No stopping point, so it either stops early or polishes forever.A numeric stop, a turn cap, and what to report.

          
"Use max effort, it's a hard one"On a single-file visual build, Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 at max can spend the whole 128k output budget thinking and return nothing.Cap visual one-shots at xhigh, set a large max_tokens, and treat stop_reason: "max_tokens" as a failed run.

          
"Use the best libraries" + "no external requests"Contradictory unless you say how assets arrive."Inline the build of X, generate textures in code, no CDN at runtime."

          
Adding features mid-runEach one is free until the page stutters."Any new effect must fit inside the current 14 ms frame budget."

          
"Grab some free models online"Non-commercial, editorial or unlicensed files slip in, and nobody can trace them later."CC0 first, CC-BY with CREDITS.md, never NC. Log URL, licence and hash per file."

          
"Model it from this concept art"Proportions measured off perspective art drift, and detail hides the drift.Orthographic views for measurement, silhouette gates before any detail.

          
"Add a cool spell effect"You get a core and some sparks. No beats, no depth, no weight.Name the beats, the layers per beat and a draw-call budget.

          
"Make the sound punchy"The model cannot hear its output, so it guesses.Envelope in ms, then an offline render with peak, loudness and a spectrogram.

          
"Build a big, detailed level"Art before play, real-world scale, dead-end trees of rooms, and nobody walks it.Beats, metrics from the controller, a layout graph with a loop, grey blockout, reachability checks, then art.

          
"Double-check everything" or "use a subagent to verify"Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 already check their work, so this adds cost, not accuracy. Sonnet at xhigh or max may add review rounds on its own.Name the checks once (frames, console, seam, soak test) and the completion condition. Use a critic only for taste, blind, against a reference.

          
"Take a screenshot to confirm it works"A frame that is not blank proves pixels arrived, not that the scene is right. Hidden tabs and unsettled exposure make it worse.Freeze state through test hooks, warm up, capture, then check a stated claim against a reference or a number.

          
"Use the best model available"The agent picks from memory: a retired model, a non-commercial checkpoint, or an unbudgeted API loop.Ask the route, use the named pick for it, confirm the id from the provider list, cap spend, and log terms and cost per file.

          
"Use the newest version"Newer releases can carry stricter licences or ship API only. Qwen-Image 2.1 went non-commercial where 2512 was Apache 2.0.Pin model and version, read the LICENSE file at download, and log both in the manifest.

          
"Use Depth Anything 3"One family, two licences. DA3-LARGE and GIANT are non-commercial, while Mono-Large, Metric-Large, Base and Small are Apache 2.0.Name the exact checkpoint and log its licence. A model family is not a licence.

          
"Make it in Unity" or "Use Unreal, it looks AAA"The agent cannot see the editor. Binary assets and editor-only steps break its build, run and look loop, and the licence changes with revenue.Pick by target platform and by what the agent can build, test and screenshot from a terminal. Name the version and the CLI or MCP route.

          
"Generate a 12-frame sprite sheet"One image holding a whole grid drifts identity, overlaps poses and falls off the grid. Longer rows add duplicates, not in-betweens.One row per state at 4 to 6 frames, or animate one still with a video model, find the loop, and pack with a manifest.

          
"Mirror it for the left-facing sprites"A sword, watch or logo on one side swaps hands, and any lettering reads backwards.Mirror only symmetric characters. Draw the left-facing views for anything one-sided and check each item's side.

          
"Make a showreel, go all out"It tests the engine, not an idea. Many people reused the same line, so the results rhyme.Add a reference, a story or a state list, and a critique loop that scores frames before the full render.

          
"Narrate this video"The voiceover drifts into the model's tics: short punchy sentences and stacks of numbers.Give the narrator a persona and pace, and have a critic compare the transcript with the script.

          
"Auto-rig the character"Learned riggers get the body mostly right and break at fingers, shoulders and clothing, and nobody checks.Auto-rig as a first pass, then weight gates and an extreme-pose sheet, with Rigify as the fallback.

          
"Keep the character consistent"Adjectives pile up and the face still drifts.Climb the ladder: reference, style reference, control map, LoRA. Keep the baseline prompt byte-identical.

          
"Put the title in the image"Garbled or restyled lettering baked into the art.Leave a quiet zone and set every word in code.

          
"Build it from scratch"A hand-rolled camera, particle system or controller with the bugs a library fixed years ago.Name the library to start from (see each section's reuse list), pin the version, log the licence.

          
"Make a fun game"Feature lists with no loop, and nobody decides what fun means.A fun target from MDA, a one-sentence core loop, three grey prototypes, bot and human playtests.

          
"Draw a nice illustration"Centred clip art with outlines and a purple gradient.Composition on thirds, a palette with roles, value pass first, and a render the model looks at.

          
"Make it look amazing"No reference, so the model picks its default style and hue-only contrast.A reference board, a style frame, a palette with roles and greyscale checks.

          
"Use physics for the player"A dynamic body that tips, slides and changes with frame rate.A kinematic controller on a fixed timestep, jump set by height and time to apex.

          
"Make it run faster"Guessing, so effort goes into the wrong cost.A benchmark path, p95 frame time, one change at a time, before and after numbers.

          
"Add bloom"One NaN pixel from a bad normal or Fresnel term spreads through the bloom blur into a black block, and it may show only after deploy.Clamp non-finite pixels before bloom, fix the source term, and soak-test long enough to cross a day-night cycle.

          
"Make the enemies smart"You get perfect aim, instant detection and no way to tell why they act.Name the decision model and states, add perception limits, a telegraph, barks and a debug overlay.

          
"Generate infinite random levels"Unseeded, unvalidated, and every output feels the same.Seeded PRNG, structure before geometry, reject-and-regenerate checks, and a contact sheet of many seeds.

          
"Make it feel juicy"Every effect at maximum on every event, nothing stands out, and there is no off switch.A feedback list per event, intensity scaling, starting numbers, and reduce-shake and reduce-flash toggles.

          
"Use a tween library for the shake"It runs on its own clock and ignores pause, hitstop and slow motion.Springs and trauma inside the fixed timestep, or drive the tween engine from the game loop.

          
"Screen-record the animation to MP4"Busy machines drop and repeat frames, and audio drifts.Seek every frame (HyperFrames, Remotion, or a seek(t) page), then encode.

          
Pasting someone's prompt as contextInstructions inside pasted text can get followed.Wrap it in tags and say which parts to borrow.
